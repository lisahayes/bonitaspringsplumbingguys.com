Signs It's Time to Replace, Not Repair

If you're weighing whether to fix it one more time or just replace it, you're probably tired of the guessing. A ten-year-old tank that's already had a repair or two doesn't usually need a fourth diagnosis — it needs a straight answer on whether it's worth saving at all.

A water heater past ten years old is usually a better candidate for replacement than another patch job, especially one that's already had a repair or two. Rust-colored water is one sign. So is a tank that's visibly rusting or leaking at the seams. Rising energy bills from declining efficiency are another. All point to a unit on its way out, not just temporarily glitching.

What Happens If You Keep Patching It

A tank near the end of its life doesn't fail on a schedule — it fails when it's least convenient.

Each repair buys less time

A part swap on a corroding tank fixes the symptom, not the tank. The next failure often comes faster than the last one did.

A full tank failure means real water damage

A 40-50 gallon tank that finally splits doesn't leak slowly — it empties, often in a garage next to stored items and drywall.

You end up paying for both

A repair right before a failure is money spent on a unit you replace anyway, plus whatever the flood damages on the way out.

Sizing It Right for This Market

A tankless unit sized for a seasonal two-bedroom condo in Pelican Landing is a different job than sizing one for a full-time family home in Bonita Bay. We factor in household size, fixture count, and whether the home runs occupied year-round or sits empty for stretches. That changes both the right unit size and the maintenance plan going forward. If you're still weighing tank versus tankless, we'll walk through the tradeoffs specific to your household during the visit.

Hard water from the area's limestone aquifer also plays into the decision. Tankless units need periodic descaling here more than they would in a soft-water market. We'll walk you through what that maintenance actually looks like before you commit to one. Homes closer to the Gulf, out toward Barefoot Beach Preserve, feel the salt-air side of that equation the most.

What a Replacement Includes

A replacement isn't just swapping one tank for another. We check everything that touches the new unit's performance and code compliance before we call the job done.

  • Remove and dispose of the old unit properly
  • Install the new one to current Florida plumbing code
  • Check the expansion tank, shutoff valve, and venting
  • Flag and explain any code-required upgrades before doing the work

Patterns We See on Calls Like This

Common on second repair calls

A frequent pattern is a homeowner calling for what they think is a second small repair, only to find the tank itself has started rusting from the inside. At that point, another repair usually isn't the more cost-effective choice — the part might hold, but the tank underneath it won't. We'll always say so plainly rather than sell a repair that just delays the same call six months later.

Frequently Asked Questions

Tank or tankless: which is better for a Bonita Springs home?

It depends on household size, hot water demand, and whether the home is full-time or seasonal. Tankless units save space and can last longer with proper descaling, but they need periodic maintenance to handle the area's hard water. We'll walk through both options for your specific situation.

How long does a water heater replacement take?

A straightforward like-for-like tank swap often takes a few hours. A conversion to tankless, or a job that needs code updates like a new expansion tank or updated venting, takes longer. We'll give you a specific estimate before starting.

Do you haul away the old unit?

Yes, removal and disposal of the old water heater is included with a replacement.
